On November 2, the comment window closed on the Canadian proposal. Most practitioners never saw it go by. That is normal for an exposure draft, and it is also the problem. By the time a revised standard lands in the Handbook, the time to shape it is gone, and the time to get ready is short.

Here's the thing. These proposals are about one gap. The old evidence rules were written for a paper world. Your files are not paper anymore. The standards are trying to catch up to the work you already do.

In this post, you'll learn what the Canadian board proposed, which three standards change, what the new idea of audit evidence looks like, how technology fits in, and what to do before any of it is final.

## LINKEDIN POST

The rules for what counts as audit evidence are getting a rewrite, and most people missed it.

On November 2 the comment window closed on the Canadian proposal to revise CAS 500, 330 and 520. Those are the three standards sitting under every audit file.

The short version. Audit evidence is being redefined for a digital file. Relevance and reliability move to the centre, especially for data that came from outside the client. Technology is handled by principle, not by name, so you can use your tools as long as you can stand behind the data under them. And professional skepticism gets written in louder.

Nothing is in force yet. There is no effective date. That makes right now the cheap time to get ready instead of scrambling later.

Here is what changes and what to do about it.
