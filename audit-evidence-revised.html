Audit evidence is being rewritten. Here's what's coming

GM Gurjot Mann, CPA  ·  Nov 15, 2026  ·  8 min read

The rules that decide what counts as audit evidence are getting a rewrite. Not a tweak. A rebuild of three standards you touch on every file.

On November 2, the comment window closed on the Canadian proposal. Most practitioners never saw it go by. That is normal for an exposure draft, and it is also the problem. By the time a revised standard lands in the Handbook, the time to shape it is gone, and the time to get ready is short.

The old evidence rules were written for a paper world. Your files are not paper anymore. You pull ledgers from the cloud, test full populations, and lean on data that came from outside the client. The standards are trying to catch up to the work you already do.

In this post, you'll learn what the Canadian board proposed, which three standards change, what the new idea of audit evidence looks like, how technology fits in, and what to do on your files before any of it is final.

What actually happened, and when

The short version

The Auditing and Assurance Standards Board put out an exposure draft called Proposed Revisions to the Canadian Auditing Standards for Audit Evidence and Risk Response. It revises three standards at once. Comments were due November 2, 2026.

The Canadian proposals follow the international ones. The IAASB released its drafts to revise the matching international standards in August 2026, and its own comment window runs to December 15, 2026. So the Canadian board moved first on the clock, and the international board is still open.

The three standards on the table

Three standards, and you use all three on every audit.

  • CAS 500, Audit Evidence. The core one. It sets what evidence is and how you judge it
  • CAS 330, The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks. How your procedures answer the risks you found
  • CAS 520, Analytical Procedures. When a comparison or a trend can stand as evidence

Change CAS 500 and you touch the base layer under every test you run. That is why this matters more than a single-standard update.

These are proposals, not rules yet

Say this part to anyone who asks. Nothing here is in force. There is no effective date. The board asked for comments, the comments are in, and the standard gets redrafted before it is issued.

So this is not a "redo your templates this week" post. It is a "know what is coming so the final version isn't a surprise" post. The firms that read exposure drafts are the ones that aren't scrambling a year later.

Why the old evidence rules feel dated

CAS 500 was built for a world of invoices, signatures, and confirmations you could hold. Reliability had a simple ladder. Original beat a copy. Outside the company beat inside. Direct beat indirect.

That ladder still helps. But look at a real file now.

  • Your trial balance comes straight out of a cloud accounting system, not a printed GL
  • You test the whole population of journal entries, not a sample of forty
  • You pull bank data through a feed, not a stamped paper statement
  • You compare the client against industry data that came from a third party
  • You use analytics software that flags the odd entries before you open the file

None of that is paper. A lot of it is information the client never typed. The standard never named any of it. Auditors have been stretching old words to cover new work, and the board wants to close the gap on purpose instead of by habit.

What the proposals change

CAS 500: a new definition of audit evidence

The headline change is the definition itself. The board wants to update what audit evidence means so it fits a digital file. The point is to stop forcing modern evidence through language written for a filing cabinet.

With that comes a sharper focus on the purpose of each procedure. Not "what did I do," but "what was I trying to prove, and did this get me there." That sounds obvious. On a rushed file it is the first thing that slips.

Relevance and reliability move to the centre

The proposals lean harder on two questions you already ask. Is this information relevant to what I am testing, and is it reliable enough to trust.

The second one gets more weight because of where data comes from now. A number pulled from an external source is not reliable just because it looks clean and arrived in a spreadsheet. You still have to know where it came from, whether it is complete, and whether anything could have changed it on the way in. The draft pushes you to show that thinking, not assume it.

CAS 330: a tighter link between risk and response

CAS 330 is about matching your work to your risks. The revisions aim to connect the pieces more clearly. Risk assessment, the response, and the final call on whether you have enough good evidence are meant to read as one chain, not three separate sections that never talk to each other.

For reviewers, this is the familiar complaint in standard form. The risk is high in the planning memo and the testing looks the same as last year. The draft wants the response to actually track the risk.

CAS 520: analytical procedures get clearer

CAS 520 covers analytics as evidence. The revision clarifies how an analytical procedure sits next to tests of controls and substantive tests, so it is clearer when a trend or a ratio can carry real weight and when it is just a sense check.

This is the one that rewards firms using data tools well. If your analytics are built on reliable data and a real expectation, they can do more of the lifting. If they are a loose plausibility glance, the draft makes that limit plain.

Professional skepticism, written in louder

Running through all of it is professional skepticism. The proposals reinforce it across the audit, not as a line in the planning file but as something you apply while you weigh each piece of evidence. More data does not mean less doubt. Sometimes it means more, because a clean-looking dataset can hide the problem better than a messy one.

The technology question, handled on purpose

Here's the choice the board made, and it is the smart one. The standards do not name the technology.

The approach is principles-based, not tool-specific. No rule that says "when you use this kind of software, do these three things." The reason is simple. Name a tool in a standard and the standard is dated the moment the tool changes. By writing to principles, the rules are meant to hold whether you are using today's analytics or something that doesn't exist yet.

What the proposals do instead is give you judgment room, with the guardrails attached. Use the tools. Just be ready to show that the data behind them is relevant and reliable, that you understood what the tool did, and that you stayed skeptical about the output.

That lands on two things Canadian practitioners deal with daily.

  • Automated tools and techniques. The analytics and testing software already in your files. The draft treats these as normal, not exotic, which is a quiet but real shift
  • External information sources. Industry benchmarks, pricing feeds, third-party data. Useful, and only as good as what you know about where they came from

What this means for a Canadian file

Bigger firms and smaller files land differently

If your firm already runs full-population testing and real analytics, a lot of this reads as a description of what you do. The gain is that the standard finally backs it. The cost is more explicit support for the data you feed those tools.

If your files are smaller and lean on classic substantive work, the change is gentler. The core steps hold. The nudge is to be sharper about reliability when any part of your evidence came from outside the client or out of a system you did not see built.

Reviews and other engagements, the direction of travel

CAS applies to audits. Your review files run under CSRE 2400 and are not part of this project. But do not file this under "audit only and forget it."

The thinking behind it, which is that evidence is digital now and reliability is the real question, does not stop at the audit line. When the profession rebuilds how it treats evidence, that view tends to flow into review and other work over time. Reading this now is how you stay ahead of where every engagement is heading.

A short list for the file

You cannot update a standard that isn't final. You can get your habits ready.

  • Know where your data comes from. For each feed or dataset, know the source, how it gets in, and what could change it
  • Separate real analytics from a sense check. Be honest about which of your procedures actually carries evidence weight
  • Tie the response to the risk. On your next planning memo, check that a high risk produces work that looks different
  • Write down the reliability call. When evidence comes from outside the client, note why you trust it, in the file, not in your head
  • Keep skepticism on the clean files too. The tidy dataset deserves the same doubt as the messy one

What to do now

You have a window before any of this is law. Use it for low-cost moves.

  • Read the exposure draft. Even a skim tells you where the final standard is heading
  • Watch the international clock. The IAASB window runs to December 15, 2026, so the global shape is still forming
  • Flag it with your standards lead. One person should own tracking this to the final version
  • Don't rebuild templates yet. It is a draft. Redoing your methodology now means redoing it again when the wording changes
  • Have the data conversation with clients. The more of their records that come from systems and feeds, the sooner reliability becomes your question to answer

The bottom line

  • Three standards are being revised at once. CAS 500, CAS 330, and CAS 520, the base layer under your audit work
  • The Canadian comment window closed November 2, 2026. The international one runs to December 15, 2026
  • Nothing is in force yet. There is no effective date, so this is about readiness, not a template redo
  • Evidence is being redefined for a digital file. Relevance and reliability move to the centre, especially for data from outside the client
  • Technology is handled by principle, not by name. Use your tools, and be ready to stand behind the data under them
  • Skepticism gets louder. A clean dataset is not the same as a trustworthy one
