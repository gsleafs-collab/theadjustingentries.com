The CRA deadlines are moving. Here's what changes when

GM Gurjot Mann, CPA  ·  Nov 22, 2026  ·  9 min read

Every tax season runs on the same few dates. April 30. June 15. Ninety days to object. A $3,000 instalment line. Budget 2026 wants to move almost all of them.

The budget was tabled on November 5, and the House passed the budget motion on November 19, 162 to 151. A Notice of Ways and Means Motion followed on November 20. None of the tax changes below are law yet. But they are on a clear track, and most of them change how you plan a season, word a client letter, or schedule your staff.

Here's the thing most headlines missed. The new May 15 deadline is a gift for employees and a squeeze for your firm. Your self-employed clients lose their June 15 filing date. Every T1 lands on the same day.

In this post, you'll learn what moves, the year each change starts, who it helps, the one change that removes a safety net, and what to do before the first new deadline arrives.

Where things stand right now

Proposed, not law

Say this part clearly to clients. The House vote on November 19 approved the budget plan. It did not pass the tax law.

The changes still need a budget implementation bill. That bill goes through committee and votes, and it can be amended on the way. The last one took months. The second bill for Budget 2025 wasn't tabled until May of this year and is still moving through Parliament, according to The Canadian Press.

So treat the dates below as the plan. Build your calendar around them. Don't promise a client a result that depends on the bill passing unchanged.

Nothing changes for 2026 returns

Clients will ask if this changes the return they file next spring. It doesn't.

The returns you file in spring 2027 still run on the old dates. April 30 to file and pay for most people. June 15 to file for the self-employed, with payment still due April 30. The first year on the new calendar is 2027, filed in 2028.

The new May 15 deadline

One date for every individual

For 2027 and later years, every individual files and pays by May 15. That includes self-employed people and their spouses or common-law partners. The budget pegs the savings at $230 million in interest and penalties over five years.

Here's how it lands on different clients:

  • Employees and retirees get 15 more days. April 30 becomes May 15
  • Self-employed clients pay 15 days later, which helps. But they file a month earlier. June 15 becomes May 15
  • Their spouses or partners move from June 15 to May 15 as well

What it does to your season

Right now most firms run two waves. The employee and retiree returns go out by April 30. The business-income returns follow by June 15.

That second wave disappears. Every T1 is due the same day.

There is one upside. For clients with a December 31 corporate year-end, the T2 is still due June 30. Today the last T1s are due two weeks before that. Under the new date, you get about six weeks between the two. But the T1 work that used to spread from April to June now lands in one spike.

Jamie Golombek of CIBC Private Wealth said the change "makes a lot of sense." He also said it "will put a lot of pressure on my friends at the accounting firms," as reported by Investment Executive. That's the honest read.

A quick example

Priya runs a bookkeeping practice as a sole proprietor. For her 2026 return, she can file by June 15, 2027, but her balance is due April 30, 2027.

For her 2027 return, she files and pays by May 15, 2028. She gets two more weeks to pay. You get one less month to prepare her return. If her records usually show up in late May, that has to change by 2028.

A full year to object

From 90 days to one year

Today a notice of objection is due 90 days after the notice of assessment. Individuals and graduated rate estates get the later of 90 days or one year after the filing due date.

The budget extends this. For most taxpayers, the deadline becomes one year after the notice of assessment is sent. For individuals and graduated rate estates, it's the later of that date or two years after the filing due date.

Brian Ernewein of KPMG put it simply in Investment Executive: "Ninety days can be awfully tight." Anyone who has chased a client for documents after a reassessment knows he's right.

The safety net goes away

But here's the catch, and it's the part to remember. Today, if you miss the deadline, you can ask for an extension. Fasken and Baker Tilly both report that the budget removes that extension process.

So the deadline gets longer, but it also gets harder. Miss the new one and there's no application to fall back on. Your diary system matters more, not less.

When it starts

It applies to assessments sent on or after the day that's three months after Royal Assent. So it depends on when the bill passes. Until then, keep using 90 days. Don't let a client sit on a reassessment because of a rule that isn't in force.

Fewer clients paying instalments

The threshold jumps to $7,500

Right now, a client pays instalments if their net tax owing is over $3,000 this year and in either of the two years before. In Quebec the federal threshold is $1,800.

The budget raises those to $7,500 and $4,500 for Quebec. Both get indexed to inflation and rounded to the nearest $500. It applies to tax years starting on or after January 1, 2028.

McMillan's summary of the budget says nearly 1.1 million individuals and 185,000 small incorporated businesses would no longer pay by instalments.

Think of a retired client with a pension and a RRIF who owes about $5,000 each spring. Today they get instalment reminders and interest charges if they ignore them. From 2028, they just pay the balance when they file.

One instalment for the self-employed

There's a second change for clients whose main income is business, professional, or commission income. Instead of four payments, they can make one payment by December 31. It equals two-thirds of their combined federal and provincial net tax owing. The rest is due on the balance-due date.

Farmers and fishers already work this way. The budget extends it to the self-employed from the 2028 year.

For a lot of sole proprietors, this is the change they'll actually feel. One year-end payment is easier to plan around than four dates they keep forgetting.

Less foreign property reporting

The T1135 threshold doubles

Form T1135 is required when the total cost of a taxpayer's specified foreign property goes over $100,000 at any time in the year. The budget raises that to $200,000.

The simplified reporting method also moves. Today it's for totals under $250,000. It moves to under $500,000.

Both apply to tax years ending after 2026. For individuals, that means the 2027 year.

Who drops off

Investment Executive reports the change exempts about one-third of current filers. Think of the client with a $150,000 US brokerage account, or a modest condo in Florida held personally. They file now. On the new rule, they likely don't.

Two things to keep in mind:

  • It's cost, not market value. Same test as today. A property that grew in value doesn't push a client over
  • Foreign income is still taxable. Dropping the form doesn't drop the reporting of the income on the return

Smaller changes to put on your list

Flat amounts instead of receipts

The budget adds optional flat amounts for some claims that eat up receipt-sorting time. Per Baker Tilly's summary:

  • Moving expenses: $1,000 per eligible move
  • Labour mobility deduction for tradespeople: $1,500 a year
  • Home accessibility tax credit: $1,000, once per lifetime
  • Adoption expense credit: $1,200 per child
  • Clergy residence: $1,000 per eligible month, up to $12,000 a year
  • Medical expenses: $1,000 a year for gluten-free food for celiac disease, $500 for incontinence products, and $2,000 for service dog care

These are optional. If the real costs are higher and the receipts are there, claim the real costs. Most start in 2027. The gluten-free amount starts in 2026.

Less paperwork for medical expenses

From 2026, four kinds of medical expenses won't need a prescription anymore. For 16 others, a certification of the medical condition replaces the prescription. That's a real time saver for clients with ongoing care costs.

Penalties get bigger and smarter

Here's the less friendly part. Many fixed-dollar penalties rise two or three times, depending on when they were first set. Medium entities pay three times the base penalty. Large ones pay eight times. A repeat failure within three years doubles the base.

The late filing penalty for a section 85 election goes to $300 a month, up to $24,000, for elections after 2027. Most of these changes apply to tax years ending after 2027.

Big tax debts go to the credit bureaus

The CRA would be able to report tax and GST/HST debts over $50,000 to credit bureaus. It only covers debts past the objection and appeal stage, and only after 30 days' written notice. This is set to apply on Royal Assent.

For the client who has been ignoring a large balance, that's a new reason to make a payment plan.

What to do now

You don't need to change much for this season. You do need to start planning for 2028.

  • Keep 2026 on the old dates. April 30 and June 15 still apply to the returns you file in 2027
  • Plan for one T1 spike. Starting in spring 2028, all your T1s are due May 15. Think about staffing and when you ask for records
  • Move self-employed clients earlier. Tell them now that their records need to show up sooner in 2028
  • Keep using 90 days for objections. Until the new rule is in force, the old deadline and extension process still apply
  • Flag T1135 filers near $200,000. Some will drop off for 2027. Note it in the file so nobody files a form out of habit
  • Update engagement letters and reminders. Deadline language in templates will be wrong for 2027
  • Watch the implementation bill. Dates can shift before it passes

The bottom line

  • Nothing is law yet. The budget motion passed November 19, but the tax changes need their own bill
  • 2026 returns keep the old dates. The new calendar starts with the 2027 tax year
  • May 15 for every individual. Employees get more time. Self-employed clients lose June 15
  • One year to object, with no extension. Longer window, but no safety net if you miss it
  • Instalments start at $7,500 from 2028. And the self-employed can make one payment by December 31
  • T1135 starts at $200,000. About a third of current filers drop off
  • Penalties go up for repeat and larger filers. The section 85 late filing penalty jumps to $300 a month
